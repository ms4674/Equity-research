# Key Points & Rebuttals: "Look How Big Is My Team" (Nicolas Bustamante)

**Source:** [Tweet](https://x.com/nicbstme/status/2023501562480644501) | [Article](https://nicolasbustamante.substack.com/p/look-how-big-is-my-team)

## Summary Table: Strongest Competitive Points & Rebuttals

| # | **Key Point (Article)** | **Strength** | **Rebuttal / Counterargument** |
|---|-------------------------|--------------|--------------------------------|
| 1 | **Headcount is a vanity metric** — Paul Graham: visitors should be "surprised how few people you have" at a successful startup. Counting employees is like counting followers or fundraising amounts. | ★★★★★ | **Nuanced:** Revenue per employee has its own limitations. Modern work involves contractors, agencies, AI systems, and offshore vendors not captured in headcount—distorting benchmarks. AI-native companies (e.g., Cursor, Lovable) now exceed $1M revenue/employee, making comparisons misleading. |
| 2 | **Small teams have competitive advantages:** Speed, better communication, engagement, higher profits to reinvest, improved productivity. | ★★★★★ | **Context-dependent:** Small teams excel at *breakthrough innovation* and high-uncertainty situations. Large teams excel at *optimization and execution at scale*—when problems are well-defined and can be parallelized. Nature study: "Large teams develop, small teams disrupt." |
| 3 | **Ringelmann effect** — Individual productivity decreases as group size increases; small teams avoid this. | ★★★★☆ | **Refined, not debunked:** Effect is real but *non-linear*. Performance drops sharply from 1→2–3 members, then levels off. Modular project structures can mitigate coordination costs. Some breakthroughs (LIGO, CERN Higgs) required 1,000+ researchers. |
| 4 | **Hiring Death Cycle** — Startups facing problems hire more people → problems get harder → more hires. "Next hire fallacy": believing the next recruit will solve everything. | ★★★★★ | **Different framing:** The issue isn't hiring per se but *alignment and process*. Fast hiring (5-day decisions) closes candidates 3x more than slow (2-week) processes. Early-stage startups have 6–12 months runway—waiting for "perfect" hires can waste critical time. |
| 5 | **Better metric: ARR/FTE** — For SaaS in $10–20M revenue range, median ARR per FTE = $138,889. Focus on revenue growth outpacing cost growth. | ★★★★☆ | **Criticisms:** (1) Doesn't account for contractors, AI, vendors. (2) Can rise while CAC or NRR deteriorate. (3) Too broad for valuation decisions. (4) Early-stage startups should prioritize product-market fit over short-term revenue extraction. |
| 6 | **Headcount constraints drive creativity and innovation** — Scarcity forces smarter solutions. | ★★★★☆ | **Partially supported:** Research confirms small teams produce more *disruptive* ideas. But large teams receive more immediate attention and citations; complex problems often require diverse expertise and interdisciplinary collaboration. |
| 7 | **Speed as competitive advantage** — Small teams move faster; startups leverage this against incumbents. | ★★★★★ | **Agreed with caveat:** True for discovery, pivoting, and early-stage execution. Once product-market fit is established, *execution capacity* becomes the constraint—larger teams can tackle multiple priorities in parallel. |

---

## Strongest Competitive Points (Ranked)

1. **Speed as competitive advantage** — Universally acknowledged; small teams move faster with less coordination overhead.
2. **Hiring Death Cycle / Next hire fallacy** — Powerful critique of reflexive hiring; rebuttals focus on *how* to hire, not whether the cycle exists.
3. **Headcount as vanity metric** — Strong; better metrics exist, though ARR/FTE has its own limitations.
4. **Ringelmann effect** — Scientifically grounded; rebuttals refine rather than reject.
5. **Small teams for innovation** — Supported by research; rebuttals add that large teams excel at development and scale.
