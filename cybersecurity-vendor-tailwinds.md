# Cyberattacks in Wartime & Tailwinds for Cybersecurity Vendors vs. Hyperscalers

## 1. Historical Pattern: War Accelerates Cyber Spending

### Russia-Ukraine War (2022-Present)

The February 2022 invasion marked a turning point for cybersecurity budgets globally. Cyberattacks were embedded into the kinetic conflict from day one — wiper malware (HermeticWiper, WhisperGate) hit Ukrainian government and banking infrastructure hours before the ground invasion. The spillover risk to Western enterprises (echoing NotPetya's $10B+ in collateral damage in 2017) triggered an immediate wave of defensive spending.

**Revenue acceleration across pure-play vendors in the war's first year (CY2022):**

| Vendor       | FY Revenue   | YoY Growth | Notes                                     |
|--------------|-------------|------------|-------------------------------------------|
| CrowdStrike  | $1.45B (FY23) | ~54%      | Record net new ARR of $217M in Q4 FY22    |
| SentinelOne  | $204.8M (FY22) | ~120%   | Fastest-growing publicly traded cyber vendor |
| Zscaler      | $1.09B (FY22) | ~62%      | Zero trust demand surged on remote work + war |
| Fortinet     | $4.42B (FY22) | ~32%      | Strong firewall + OT security demand       |

By late CY2022/early CY2023, macro headwinds elongated sales cycles (CrowdStrike noted deal compression in Q3 FY23), but the structural uplift in security budgets persisted. The war permanently raised baseline threat perception for enterprises and governments alike.

### Estonia (2007) — The First State-Level Cyberwar

Russian-linked DDoS attacks crippled Estonian banking, media, and government infrastructure for weeks. This was the catalyst for NATO establishing the Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence (CCDCOE) in Tallinn and eventually recognizing cyberspace as a formal warfare domain in 2016.

### Israel-Hamas Conflict (2023-Present)

The October 7, 2023 attacks triggered a multi-phase cyber escalation:
- **Phase 1 (Oct 2023):** Iranian state-aligned actors launched opportunistic hack-and-leak operations against Israeli targets
- **Phase 2 (Mid-Oct):** Escalation to wiper malware, ICS targeting, and influence campaigns
- **Phase 3 (Nov+ onward):** Persistent, destructive operations across government, finance, tech, and defense sectors

In 2019, Israel conducted a kinetic airstrike on a Hamas facility in response to cyberattacks — a rare precedent for military escalation in response to cyber operations.

### Georgia (2008), Colonial Pipeline (2021), SolarWinds (2020)

Each major cyber incident — whether war-linked or not — produced a measurable step-function increase in government and enterprise security budgets:
- **SolarWinds (Dec 2020):** Biden administration allocated $750M specifically for affected agencies, plus $500M in additional federal cybersecurity funding
- **Colonial Pipeline (May 2021):** Executive Order on Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity, mandatory incident reporting for critical infrastructure
- **Georgia (2008):** Russian cyberattacks coordinated with the ground invasion set the template later replicated at scale in Ukraine

## 2. Current Threat & Budget Environment (2025-2026)

The geopolitical environment is the most cyber-hostile on record:

- **State actors (China, Russia, Iran, North Korea)** have shifted from disruption toward persistent access — targeting identity systems, cloud environments, and edge infrastructure
- **AI-enhanced attacks:** Deepfake-enabled fraud up 10x, synthetic identity fraud up 300% in early 2025
- **Pentagon cyber budget:** $15.1B in FY2026 NDAA (+4.1% YoY), with $9.1B for core cybersecurity operations
- **NATO pledge (June 2025):** 5% GDP defense spending target by 2035, with **1.5% of GDP specifically earmarked for cybersecurity and critical infrastructure** — potentially driving 40% of global cybersecurity sector growth and pushing the market past $500B annually by 2032
- **Global market:** $281B in 2025, projected $352B by 2030 (9.1% CAGR)

## 3. Tailwinds for Pure-Play Cybersecurity Vendors

### 3a. What Works in Their Favor

**1. Urgency favors best-of-breed.** When a CISO is responding to an active nation-state threat or a board is reacting to a headline-grabbing breach, the buying decision tilts toward the vendor with the deepest expertise in that attack surface — not the vendor that also sells email and spreadsheets. War periods compress evaluation cycles and reward specialization.

**2. Government/defense budgets are earmarked for cyber specialists.** The $15.1B Pentagon cyber budget and NATO's 1.5%-of-GDP cyber commitment flow disproportionately to companies with FedRAMP, IL5+, and NATO-specific certifications. CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and Fortinet have deep government contract relationships that hyperscalers cannot easily replicate in classified environments.

**3. Product velocity and innovation cycles.** Pure-play vendors ship security-specific innovation faster. CrowdStrike's Falcon platform, Palo Alto's Cortex XSIAM, Zscaler's Zero Trust Exchange, and SentinelOne's AI-native architecture iterate on threat intelligence at a cadence that hyperscaler security teams — which are one division among many — struggle to match.

**4. Vendor trust and independence.** Enterprises running multi-cloud environments (AWS + Azure + GCP) need a security layer that is cloud-agnostic. A hyperscaler's security product creates lock-in and introduces conflict-of-interest concerns (the cloud provider is both the infrastructure and the security auditor). This independence is a structural moat for pure-plays.

**5. Revenue growth remains strong.** Q3 2024 data shows the top 16 publicly listed cybersecurity vendors averaged 14.2% revenue growth, with leaders like CrowdStrike (29%), Zscaler (26%), CyberArk (26%), SentinelOne (28%), and Cloudflare (28%) significantly outpacing the market.

### 3b. What Works Against Them (Hyperscaler Headwinds)

**1. Microsoft's $20B+ security business is the elephant in the room.** Microsoft bundles security across M365 E5, Azure Defender, Sentinel (SIEM), Entra ID, and Intune. For budget-constrained organizations, "good enough" security included in an existing enterprise agreement is a powerful substitute. Microsoft processes 78 trillion security signals daily and tracks 1,500+ threat groups — scale that pure-plays cannot match.

**2. Google's $32B Wiz acquisition (cleared Nov 2025).** Google is signaling that cloud security is a first-class competitive weapon in the hyperscaler war. Wiz's CNAPP capabilities, integrated into GCP, will pressure cloud-native security vendors (Orca, Lacework, etc.) and even CrowdStrike's cloud security module.

**3. Platform consolidation is real.** Palo Alto Networks' platformization strategy and CrowdStrike's module expansion are effectively the pure-play response to hyperscaler bundling — but it creates a K-shaped market. Upper-tier platforms (CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Fortinet) command 12-18x EV/Revenue, while lower-tier point solutions compress to 3-5x. War-driven urgency helps the top-tier pure-plays but accelerates the death of smaller point solutions.

**4. Cisco + Splunk integration.** Cisco's $28B acquisition of Splunk concentrates SIEM/observability capabilities in another platform giant, further squeezing mid-tier pure-plays.

## 4. Net Assessment: Who Benefits More from Wartime Tailwinds?

| Dimension                     | Pure-Play Advantage | Hyperscaler Advantage |
|-------------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Acute threat response urgency | Strong             | Moderate              |
| Government/defense budgets    | Strong             | Moderate              |
| Multi-cloud independence      | Strong             | Weak                  |
| Product depth & innovation    | Strong             | Moderate              |
| Bundling / total cost of ownership | Weak          | Strong                |
| Data scale / signal volume    | Moderate           | Strong                |
| Platform consolidation trend  | Mixed (top-tier wins) | Strong             |
| AI-native security            | Strong (early)     | Strong (long-term)    |

### Bottom Line

**In the short-to-medium term (1-3 years), wartime and geopolitical escalation disproportionately benefits pure-play cybersecurity vendors**, particularly the top-tier platforms (CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Fortinet, Zscaler). The mechanism is straightforward:

1. **Fear drives budget unlocks.** CISOs and boards point to active nation-state threats to justify incremental spend that goes to best-of-breed solutions, not bundled-in capabilities.
2. **Government/sovereign spend is earmarked.** NATO's 1.5%-of-GDP cyber commitment and the Pentagon's $15B+ cyber budget flow through channels where pure-plays have entrenched positions and certifications.
3. **Multi-cloud is a structural moat.** As enterprises diversify across AWS, Azure, and GCP partly in response to geopolitical risk, the need for cloud-agnostic security grows — benefiting pure-plays.

**In the long term (3-5+ years), hyperscalers erode the pure-play advantage** through bundling, acquisitions (Wiz, Splunk), and AI-native security capabilities that leverage their unmatched data scale. The risk for pure-plays is that wartime urgency fades, budgets normalize, and CFOs gravitate toward consolidated platforms that reduce vendor count and total cost of ownership.

**The K-shaped outcome:** Top-tier pure-plays (CrowdStrike, Palo Alto, Zscaler) survive and thrive by becoming platforms themselves. Mid-tier and point-solution vendors face existential pressure from both hyperscaler bundling above and open-source/AI automation below. War-driven tailwinds accelerate this bifurcation — they do not prevent it.
