# What Europe Must Do by 2035 to Achieve Sovereignty in Large Language Models

## The Starting Point

Europe enters the AI race from a position of structural weakness. The EU holds less than 5% of global AI supercomputing power. Its enterprises source over 80% of their digital services from non-EU providers. It has produced only 6% of the world's notable AI models despite a strong research base. Mistral AI, the sole EU frontier model developer, has an annualised revenue run rate of $400 million -- less than 3% of what OpenAI and Anthropic generate combined. The US Big 4 technology companies plan to spend $630 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone, which is 30 times the EU's entire Gigafactory programme budget.

And yet the EU has genuine advantages it can exploit: ASML's monopoly on EUV lithography, a world-class semiconductor R&D ecosystem, 450 million consumers under a coherent regulatory framework, 24 official languages that create a natural moat for multilingual models, the largest sovereign cloud market in the world, and a regulatory environment that increasingly favours data sovereignty. The path to LLM sovereignty does not require matching the US in scale. It requires building sufficient capability that Europe can train, deploy, and govern its own frontier models for the use cases that matter most -- government, defence, healthcare, industrial automation, and critical infrastructure.

## The Seven Imperatives

**1. Deliver the AI Gigafactories on time -- or faster.** The 7-8 planned facilities with 75,000+ GPUs each are the compute backbone of EU LLM sovereignty. Construction must begin in 2028 with first operations by 2030. Any further delay widens the gap. The EU should adopt modular, refresh-ready architectures so that hardware can be upgraded every 2-3 years without rebuilding facilities. The current programme provides roughly 4-5 GW of sovereign training capacity by 2030. That is enough to train 7-8 frontier models simultaneously -- a small fraction of US capacity but sufficient for strategic autonomy if used efficiently. The EUR 20 billion InvestAI envelope, combined with the EUR 10 billion already invested in AI Factories, must be fully mobilised. Private consortia like AION (EUR 10 billion, France) and Campus AI (3 GW target, Bpifrance/Mistral/Nvidia/MGX) should be treated as complementary assets, not competitors to the public programme.

**2. Nurture at least two more frontier model developers beyond Mistral.** A single company cannot carry an entire continent's AI sovereignty. Mistral is targeting $1 billion in revenue for 2026, but it remains a startup competing against firms with $100+ billion in annual revenue. Europe needs 2-3 additional companies training frontier-class models by 2030. These could emerge from the existing research ecosystem (Hugging Face, Aleph Alpha, academic spin-offs) or through new entrants backed by the Chips Fund and Gigafactory compute access. The AI Factories' free-access tiers -- Playground, Fast Lane (50,000 GPU-hours), and Large Scale -- are a critical mechanism for democratising access and incubating the next wave of EU model developers.

**3. Implement the open-source-first procurement principle with force.** The Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) proposes that public administrations adopt an open-source-first principle for AI software procurement. This is the single most powerful demand-side lever the EU possesses. If all 27 Member State governments, EU institutions, and public-sector bodies default to EU-developed open-weight models for non-classified workloads, that creates a market floor of EUR 5-10 billion annually by 2030. It validates EU models in production, generates domain-specific fine-tuning data, and creates the flywheel that private enterprises then follow. The SEAL sovereignty framework (Levels 1-4) must be fully enacted by 2028, with SEAL-3 and SEAL-4 mandatory for sensitive government workloads, ensuring that AI inference data for public-sector applications is processed exclusively under EU jurisdiction.

**4. Fund multilingual, domain-specific model development as a strategic priority.** Europe's 24 official languages are a weakness for US-built models and a competitive advantage for EU models. The EU should allocate at least EUR 2 billion from the CADA R&D budget specifically to training high-quality multilingual foundation models that perform at frontier level across all EU languages, not just English. Domain-specific models for healthcare (EMA-compliant drug discovery), industrial automation (Industrie 4.0), financial services (ECB/SSM-aligned risk modelling), and defence/security represent verticals where EU models can achieve superiority because the training data is European and the regulatory requirements are EU-specific. EU Common Data Spaces in health, agriculture, manufacturing, and energy must be operationalised and connected to Gigafactory compute to enable this.

**5. Build the sovereign inference layer -- not just the training layer.** By 2030, inference will account for 72% of all AI infrastructure spend. The Gigafactories are designed primarily for training. Inference requires a different, distributed architecture: regional hubs of 20-100 MW near major cities and edge nodes under 10 MW at network points of presence. The CADA data centre tripling target (from ~10 GW to ~30 GW total by 2031-2033) must deliver inference-optimised capacity, not just general-purpose data centres. EU sovereign cloud providers -- OVHcloud, Scaleway, STACKIT, T-Systems -- must be scaled to offer competitive inference-as-a-service with sub-50ms latency across all EU metros. Without this, EU-trained models will run on US cloud infrastructure, undermining the sovereignty investment upstream.

**6. Close the semiconductor dependency for AI chips by 2033-2035.** The EU's 3nm open foundry (pilot 2030-2033) is essential but not sufficient on its own. EU AI chip designers -- Axelera AI for inference, SiPearl for HPC -- must reach volume production. The Chips Act 2.0 Grand Challenges and public innovation procurement must be used to validate EU-designed silicon in Gigafactory racks. A realistic target is 10-15% of Gigafactory GPU refreshes sourced from EU-designed chips by 2035. The remaining 85-90% will come from NVIDIA and AMD, but diversified sourcing (including RISC-V open architectures) reduces single-vendor dependency. The EUR 30 billion advanced foundry investment and broader EUR 120 billion semiconductor mobilisation are prerequisites for this.

**7. Retain and attract AI talent.** Europe produces world-class AI researchers but loses them to US labs offering 10-100 times more compute and substantially higher compensation. The Gigafactories directly address the compute gap. The Semiconductor Regions of Excellence label and competence centre network address the pipeline. But the EU also needs targeted visa and compensation frameworks to attract and retain top AI talent. If Europe cannot keep its best researchers, no amount of infrastructure investment will deliver sovereignty.

## The Target by 2035

If these seven imperatives are executed, the EU can realistically achieve the following by 2035: 7-8 operational AI Gigafactories providing ~6 GW of sovereign training capacity; 2-3 frontier model developers with combined revenue exceeding EUR 15 billion; EU-developed models serving 35-40% of the EU's $35 billion LLM market; open-weight EU models deployed in all 27 Member State governments; 10 GW of sovereign inference infrastructure; and 10-15% of AI accelerators in sovereign facilities designed in the EU. This is not parity with the US -- the compute gap will remain at roughly 8:1 -- but it is sufficient for genuine technological autonomy in the applications that define European security, public services, and industrial competitiveness.

# What Happens if the EU Does Not Support Sovereign LLM Development

## The Dependency Deepens

If the EU fails to execute its AI Gigafactory programme, fails to implement the CADA sovereignty framework, or allows the Chips Act 2.0 demand-side measures to stall in legislative negotiation, the consequences compound rapidly. The current trajectory -- without intervention -- leads to an EU that is a permanent consumer of US-built AI, with all the strategic, economic, and democratic vulnerabilities that entails.

## Economic Extraction at Scale

The EU LLM market is projected to grow from $2 billion in 2025 to $35 billion by 2035. Without sovereign alternatives, 75-85% of that spend -- $26-30 billion annually -- flows directly to US model providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI) and the US cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, GCP) on which they run. Over the decade 2025-2035, cumulative EU spending on US AI services could exceed $150 billion. This is not simply a trade deficit; it is the export of the economic value of European data. When a European hospital uses a US LLM for diagnostic support, the model improves from European medical data, but the improved model and its economic returns accrue to a US company. When a European manufacturer uses a US LLM for production optimisation, the proprietary operational data that makes the model valuable is captured outside EU jurisdiction. Every enterprise AI deployment on foreign infrastructure is a one-way transfer of domain knowledge that the EU can never recover.

## Loss of Regulatory Leverage

The EU AI Act is the world's most comprehensive AI regulation. But regulation without capability is an empty threat. If no European company can build a competitive frontier model, the EU becomes a rule-taker, not a rule-maker. US model providers will comply with EU regulation only to the extent that the EU market justifies the cost of compliance. As the US domestic market grows to $50+ billion and China's approaches $30 billion, the EU's $35 billion market becomes a secondary consideration. US providers may offer EU-compliant versions of their models -- as they currently do with GDPR-compliant cloud regions -- but these will be derivative products, not models designed from the ground up for European values, languages, and institutional requirements. The EU's ability to enforce meaningful AI governance erodes because there is no credible domestic alternative if a US provider decides compliance is too costly or politically inconvenient.

## Extraterritorial Exposure and the Kill Switch Risk

Under the US CLOUD Act and FISA Section 702, US authorities can compel US companies to hand over data stored or processed anywhere in the world, including on servers physically located in the EU. If European governments, healthcare systems, critical infrastructure operators, and defence agencies run their AI workloads on US-controlled models and platforms, their sensitive data is subject to US jurisdiction regardless of where the servers sit. The CADA SEAL framework addresses this by creating sovereignty tiers, but without domestic model alternatives, public-sector buyers have nowhere to migrate when sovereignty risk materialises. The geopolitical dimension is equally stark. The Trump administration's use of technology access as a foreign policy lever -- including export controls on semiconductors and threats to revoke cloud access -- demonstrated that US technology dependency is a coercive instrument. A European government that depends entirely on US LLMs for its administrative processes, intelligence analysis, and public services has accepted a structural vulnerability: the ability of a foreign government to degrade or disable core state functions through technology denial. This is not a theoretical risk. It is the explicit scenario the European Technological Sovereignty Package was designed to prevent.

## Brain Drain Becomes Permanent

Without sovereign compute infrastructure and a viable EU AI industry, the brain drain that has already hollowed out Europe's AI research capacity becomes permanent and irreversible. The pattern is well-documented: European universities produce world-class AI researchers; US labs recruit them with 10-100 times more compute, higher salaries, and the opportunity to work on frontier models. Stanford's data shows Europe produced only 6% of notable AI models in 2025 despite having roughly equal research output to the US in fundamental machine learning. Without Gigafactories and EU model developers, there is no European lab that can offer a competitive research environment. The best PhD graduates from ETH Zurich, INRIA, the Max Planck Institutes, and Cambridge will continue to move to OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Anthropic. Over time, the EU loses not only the researchers but the institutional capacity to train new ones, as supervising professors themselves leave for industry roles in the US. By 2035, Europe's AI talent pipeline would be structurally broken.

## Industrial Competitiveness Erodes

AI is becoming the foundational technology layer of every industry -- manufacturing, automotive, energy, financial services, healthcare, agriculture. The EU's industrial champions (Volkswagen, Siemens, BASF, Roche, Airbus, BNP Paribas) are integrating AI into their core operations. If they depend entirely on US LLMs for this integration, they are building their competitive future on a platform controlled by a foreign power. US model providers will serve EU industrial customers, but they will also serve those customers' US, Chinese, and global competitors. There is no mechanism by which a European manufacturer using GPT-7 for process optimisation gains a durable competitive advantage, because its competitors use the same model. The advantage accrues to the model provider, not the customer. In contrast, if EU industrial firms could train domain-specific models on sovereign infrastructure using proprietary European industrial data, the resulting models would be a genuine source of competitive differentiation. Without sovereign LLM capability, European industry becomes an AI consumer rather than an AI innovator, progressively losing the ability to control its own digital transformation.

## Democratic and Societal Consequences

LLMs are not neutral tools. They encode values, cultural assumptions, and linguistic biases from their training data and the decisions of their developers. A Europe that relies entirely on US-developed models accepts that American cultural and commercial priorities shape the AI systems used in European education, public services, media, and civic discourse. Models optimised for English-language, US-centric use cases will consistently underserve the 24 official languages of the EU, disadvantaging citizens who do not speak English as a first language. More fundamentally, the governance of AI models -- decisions about safety, content moderation, acceptable use, and alignment -- would be made in San Francisco and New York, not in Brussels, Paris, or Berlin. European citizens would have no meaningful democratic input into the AI systems that increasingly shape their access to information, government services, healthcare advice, and economic opportunity.

## The Quantified Cost of Inaction

By 2035, the cost of inaction can be summarised in five figures: $150 billion in cumulative AI spend exported to US providers; 35,000-50,000 AI researchers and engineers lost to US industry; zero frontier models trained on sovereign infrastructure; 85%+ of EU enterprise AI workloads running on foreign-controlled platforms; and a permanent 8:1 compute gap that ensures Europe can never catch up without starting the entire investment cycle from scratch. The EUR 350-450 billion that the EU plans to invest across the Chips Act 2.0, CADA, and Gigafactory programmes is substantial. But the cost of not investing is larger. It is the difference between an EU that shapes the AI era and one that is shaped by it.
